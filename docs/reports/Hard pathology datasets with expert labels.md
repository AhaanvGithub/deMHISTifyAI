# Build on MHIST, keep CONCH frozen

The best dataset for your goal is the one you already have: **MHIST**. It covers a diagnosis that pathologists genuinely disagree on, telling a harmless hyperplastic polyp (HP) from a sessile serrated lesion (SSL, a precancerous polyp). Every image comes with **how many of 7 gut pathologists voted "SSL"**, and only a handful of public datasets carry that kind of disagreement record ([MHIST](https://bmirds.github.io/MHIST/)). The pathologists agreed with each other only moderately (average kappa 0.45, where 1.0 is perfect agreement), and about **1 image in 6 was a 4-to-3 split** ([Wei et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2101.12355)). New 2026 studies show the problem has not gone away under current WHO criteria: SSL agreement is still only kappa 0.30–0.44 ([WJG 2026](https://www.wjgnet.com/1007-9327/full/v32/i42/120411.htm)). Other public datasets with per-pathologist labels do exist. Gleason2019 (prostate, 6 pathologists), Chaoyang (colon, 3 pathologists) and MITOS-ATYPIA-14 (breast, 2 pathologists) are the realistic add-ons. The most clinically dramatic hard diagnoses have no public multi-rater images you could get today: melanoma vs atypical mole, Barrett's low-grade dysplasia and breast atypia. On modelling, **keep CONCH frozen**. Compute its image features once and train small classifier heads on the vote fractions ("soft labels"). Published benchmarks show fine-tuning buys at most a few points on small, noisy datasets and sometimes loses. It also costs GPU time you lose at 6 pm Central today. It would work in the sense that matters. Raw accuracy is not where you stand out: a plain ImageNet ResNet-18 already reaches 92.7% AUC on MHIST. You stand out by building a tool that predicts *how much pathologists would disagree*, explains itself through named diagnostic criteria, and raises a trust flag on the split cases.

## A few terms, defined once

The table below defines the technical words used later, so the rest of the report can stay plain.

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Tile / patch | A small square cut from a scanned slide (here 224×224 pixels), the unit the model looks at |
| WSI | Whole-slide image: the full scanned glass slide, often gigabytes per slide |
| Kappa (κ) | An agreement score that discounts agreement expected by chance. Roughly: below 0.4 is poor to fair, 0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 good, above 0.8 near-perfect |
| Foundation model (CONCH, MUSK, UNI) | A large network pre-trained on millions of pathology images (and text, for CONCH/MUSK). It turns each tile into a list of numbers (an *embedding*) that captures what it looks like |
| Frozen | You use the model as-is and never change its weights |
| Linear probe / MLP head | A tiny classifier trained on top of frozen embeddings. Linear means one layer; MLP means 2–3 layers. Trains in seconds |
| Zero-shot | Classifying with no training at all, by comparing a tile to text prompts such as "sessile serrated lesion" |
| Fine-tuning (full / LoRA) | Changing the big model's own weights. Full fine-tuning updates all of them. LoRA adds small trainable "adapter" pieces and updates only those |
| Soft labels | Training targets that carry the vote split. A 5-of-7 SSL image becomes "71% SSL" instead of a flat "SSL" |
| Calibration (ECE) | Whether a model's stated confidence matches how often it is right. ECE (expected calibration error) measures the gap; lower is better |

## Pathologists split most where treatment decisions hinge

Agreement studies show the same pattern across organs. Pathologists agree well on clearly benign and clearly cancerous tissue. They disagree in the **"in-between" categories**, which are exactly the ones that decide treatment. The best-measured examples involve hundreds of pathologists. In breast biopsies, 115 US pathologists matched an expert panel on **only 48% of atypia cases**, against 96% for invasive cancer ([Elmore et al., JAMA 2015](https://cinj.org/sites/cinj/files/documents/Diagnostic%20Concordance%20Among%20Pathologists%20Interpreting%20Breast%20Biopsy%20Specimens.pdf)). In melanocytic skin lesions, 187 pathologists were accurate on only **25–43% of the middle categories**, from moderately atypical mole to early melanoma. They often disagreed with themselves when shown the same case months later ([Elmore et al., BMJ 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28659278/)). Barrett's low-grade dysplasia reaches only kappa 0.28 between specialists ([PubMed 11151865](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11151865/)). Cervical CIN grading sits at kappa 0.46 ([ALTS, JAMA 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11255427/)). Prostate cribriform pattern sits at kappa 0.40 among 27 specialists ([PubMed 34522463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34522463/)).

SSL versus HP belongs in this group, though its stakes are moderate rather than extreme. Calling an SSL a hyperplastic polyp means the patient's next colonoscopy is scheduled years later than it should be, because SSLs are a known route to colorectal cancer. The disagreement survives every revision of the criteria. The WHO 2019 rules now say **one clearly distorted crypt is enough** to call SSL. Even so, a 2026 study of 6 GI pathologists and 1,176 polyps found SSL kappa of 0.30–0.44. Accuracy against the reference was only **58–62%**, and agreement on "SSL with dysplasia" collapsed to kappa 0.11–0.13 ([WJG 2026](https://www.wjgnet.com/1007-9327/full/v32/i42/120411.htm); the journal lists an issue date slightly after today, so treat it as online-ahead-of-print). A second 2026 study of 13 expert GI pathologists found something useful for your tool. Agreement rose from **kappa 0.46 to 0.63 when pathologists were told the polyp's size and location** ([Booth et al., Histopathology 2026](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/despite-simplified-diagnostic-criteria-intraobserver-and-interobs/)). In other words, part of the difficulty cannot be solved from a single tile. A tool that says "this tile is ambiguous, check size and site" is giving clinically correct advice, not dodging the question.

This also answers your "good number of variables" goal. SSL diagnosis rests on a short checklist of named features that a pathologist scores by eye. The checklist covers crypt shape at the base (dilated, L-shaped or "boot" crypts), crypts growing sideways along the muscle layer, serration reaching down to the crypt base, and features of dysplasia. Your biomed teammate and their father can turn the current WHO wording into 6–10 such criteria. Each one becomes a variable the tool scores and the Lumen LLM can cite.

## Frozen CONCH with soft-label heads is the evidence-backed choice

Your hackathon slides recommend frozen weights, and the published evidence points the same way for data this small and this noisy. The most careful comparison tested four pathology foundation models on 14 datasets, using every adaptation method from linear probing to full fine-tuning and LoRA. LoRA won most often overall. But on Chaoyang, the noisy-label colon dataset most like MHIST, **a plain linear probe beat LoRA for two of the four models** (linear-probe accuracy: UNI 70.3%, Phikon 74.4%) ([Lee et al., arXiv 2410.16038](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16038v1)). Where LoRA did win, on cleaner sets such as BACH, the margin was typically a few points. Full fine-tuning was rarely best. Several groups report that it overfits or "forgets" useful general knowledge on small datasets ([arXiv 2501.11014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.11014); [arXiv 2510.23807](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.23807v1)). With noisy labels, the extra flexibility of fine-tuning tends to memorise the disagreement rather than learn from it.

The decisive argument is what frozen features make possible. Once each tile is turned into an embedding and saved to disk, every later experiment runs on a laptop CPU in seconds. That covers different heads, soft vs hard labels, multiple random seeds, calibration, and the trust-flag logic. It removes the 6 pm GPU deadline from your critical path. Soft labels are the cheapest high-value trick available, and the MHIST group itself showed why. Training on agreement-aware targets instead of flat labels cut **calibration error by almost 70%** on colorectal polyp images while keeping or improving accuracy ([Wei et al., arXiv 2201.11866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11866v1)). That group also showed that feeding training images in order from clear-cut to contested helps ([WACV 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2021/html/Wei_Learn_Like_a_Pathologist_Curriculum_Learning_by_Annotator_Agreement_for_WACV_2021_paper.html)). With MHIST, the soft target is simply votes ÷ 7.

On the CONCH vs MUSK question, CONCH is the right lead. It is approved now, it runs at MHIST's native 224 px, and its tile-level features score slightly higher in an independent 23-model benchmark (linear-probe average 81.9 vs 81.1 for MUSK) ([THUNDER, via arXiv 2607.22861](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.22861v1)). MUSK expects 384 px input, so it costs about three times as much compute per tile ([MUSK GitHub](https://github.com/lilab-stanford/MUSK)). Its licence forbids sharing derivatives, which makes a fine-tuned MUSK awkward to show publicly ([MUSK GitHub](https://github.com/lilab-stanford/MUSK)). If MUSK access arrives in time, embed MHIST with it and use it as a second "voter". Disagreement between CONCH and MUSK is itself a trust flag. Zero-shot use (text prompts only, no training) is worth keeping for the *criteria* layer, but not as the main classifier. The prompt wording alone moved one CONCH dysplasia task from AUC 0.935 to 0.736 ([Sharma et al., arXiv 2505.00134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00134)).

**Will it work?** Partly, and it is worth being clear about which part. No published paper reports CONCH or MUSK on MHIST with exact numbers, so you will be producing the first clean figure yourselves. The bar to beat is the original paper's ImageNet ResNet-18 at **92.7% AUC**, trained in about six minutes ([Wei et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2101.12355)). A frozen CONCH head will probably land near that, possibly a little above or below. Do not pitch accuracy as the win. Some of MHIST's labels are 4-to-3 coin flips, so the ceiling sits below 100%, and 1–2-point differences are noise. What *will* work, and what earlier work only partly covered, is three things. First, a model that predicts the vote split, saying "about 4 of 7 pathologists would call this SSL". Second, honest confidence. Third, a measurable result: the tool flags the contested tiles much more often than the clear ones. Fine-tuning would not improve any of those three.

## Turning criteria, papers and flags into one tool

The pieces you described fit together into one design that plays to each teammate's strengths. The **image model** (frozen CONCH plus a small head trained on soft labels) outputs a predicted SSL probability and a predicted "disagreement level". The **criteria layer** scores each tile against the 6–10 named SSL features using CONCH's text side. One option is to compare the tile embedding with prompts like "dilated crypt base" and their paraphrases. The other is to have the father mark a few dozen tiles per feature so you can train tiny per-feature heads. Either way, each criterion becomes a visible variable. The **Lumen LLM** then reads the criteria scores and retrieves supporting passages from the papers above: WHO 2019 criteria, the 2026 agreement studies, and surveillance guidelines. It writes a short, cited rationale. The **trust flags** fire when the predicted vote is near 50/50, when CONCH and MUSK disagree, when criteria scores flip across reworded prompts, or when the criteria contradict the final call. The Booth study lets one flag carry clinical weight: "ambiguous on morphology alone; polyp size and site raised expert agreement from κ 0.46 to 0.63" ([Booth et al.](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/despite-simplified-diagnostic-criteria-intraobserver-and-interobs/)).

Your evaluation then answers questions a pathologist judge cares about. Does accuracy fall as the vote gets closer (7/7 vs 6/7 vs 5/7 vs 4/7)? Do the flags concentrate on the 4/7 tiles? Is the tool's confidence calibrated (ECE and Brier score against the vote fractions)? The father's input is a rare asset here. If he reads even 50–100 MHIST tiles blind, including many 4/7 cases, he becomes an "eighth rater". You can then show where the tool, the 7-person panel and a practising pathologist agree and disagree. Treat it honestly as one reader's opinion, not ground truth. One risk to say out loud: MHIST tiles lack size and site information, and the 2026 evidence says that missing context is part of why the task is hard. Your tool should not claim to replace it.

## Conclusion

The rare thing in public pathology data is not hard images but a *record of the disagreement*. MHIST is one of the few datasets that keeps it, and that changes what your tool should aim for. The interesting output is not "SSL or not". It is "how contested is this, why, and what would settle it". The 2026 finding that size and site lift agreement turns the trust flag from a hedge into advice a clinician can act on. Frozen features are the right engineering choice for this framing, not a compromise. Your edge lies in modelling the disagreement and explaining it, and that work happens after the GPU shuts off.
